IASB Takes Up Crypto and Stablecoin Accounting: What Has Been Decided?

A new project will examine cryptocurrency measurement and whether some stablecoins could be cash equivalents

By Hendrawanto, SE., Ak., CA., CLI., ASEAN CPA, CPA., CFI. · 26 November 2026 · Baca dalam Bahasa Indonesia

The International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) decided to add a narrow-scope project on cryptoasset accounting to its work plan. The decision was taken on 19 November 2026, the final day of the IASB meeting held on 17–19 November 2026, and was supported by 12 of the 13 IASB members. Its focus is holdings of cryptocurrencies and stablecoins. According to the IFRS Foundation's explanation, the project will explore two questions: whether cryptocurrencies should be measured at fair value through profit or loss, and whether some stablecoins could qualify as cash equivalents under IAS 7 Statement of Cash Flows.

Adding a project to the work plan is not the issue of a new standard. The IASB project page shows the next milestone as Review Research, while the IFRS Foundation states that it will provide further information on the project's scope and timing as it develops its plans. Companies therefore do not need to change their accounting policies merely because they have read news about this project. What they can do now is understand the nature of the assets they hold, the basis of the treatment currently applied, and the areas that may change if standard-setting eventually produces a result.

The Project's Scope Should Be Read Narrowly

The term “cryptoassets” covers a wide range of rights and transaction patterns. The November 2026 IASB Update describes the project as addressing specific aspects of cryptoasset accounting, namely holdings of cryptocurrencies and stablecoins. The IFRS Foundation explains that this focus reflects the areas stakeholders typically identify as higher priorities. The official documents do not say that token issuance, custody services, exchanges, or other blockchain-based contracts will be addressed at the same time. Widening the scope without a basis may lead readers to misread the status of the discussion.

For cryptocurrencies, the question concerns measurement. The IFRS Foundation uses the word explore: the IASB will explore measurement at fair value through profit or loss. This is a matter under study, not a method already required of every crypto holder. For preparers, the distinction determines whether the information is used for monitoring or as a basis for current recording.

For stablecoins, the question concerns classification. The word “some” matters: the stablecoin label does not automatically make an asset cash or a cash equivalent. The holder's rights against the issuer, the redemption mechanism, convertibility, and the risk of changes in value are facts that need to be understood before any analysis. The assessment of a particular instrument still depends on that instrument's terms and evidence.

What Already Exists in the IFRS Framework?

Before this project, the IFRS Interpretations Committee issued the “Holdings of Cryptocurrencies” agenda decision in June 2019. The fact pattern it addressed was limited to a digital or virtual currency recorded on a distributed ledger that uses cryptography, that is not issued by a jurisdictional authority or other party, and that does not give rise to a contract between the holder and another party. This boundary matters: the decision cannot simply be carried over to stablecoins or to tokens that confer contractual rights.

For cryptocurrencies within that fact pattern, the Committee concluded that a holding meets the definition of an intangible asset in IAS 38 and is neither a financial asset nor cash. It then concluded that IAS 2 Inventories applies when cryptocurrencies are held for sale in the ordinary course of business; if IAS 2 does not apply, the entity applies IAS 38 Intangible Assets. An entity acting as a broker-trader should consider paragraph 3(b) of IAS 2, under which commodity broker-traders measure their inventories at fair value less costs to sell. This conclusion shows why the purpose of holding and the business model must be identified before choosing an accounting basis.

The 2019 agenda decision explains how existing standards apply to a particular fact pattern, whereas the November 2026 project opens a review of aspects considered to need further consideration. Neither should be read as if the earlier agenda decision has been withdrawn or the new project has already produced final requirements. Until there are further official developments, preparers should document the reasons for their policy choices based on the standards that apply within their entity's reporting framework.

Why the Distinction Matters in Indonesia

In Indonesia, financial statements are prepared under the Financial Accounting Standards (SAK) that apply to the entity concerned. Developments at the IASB do not automatically become changes to PSAK; readers should follow the steps of IAI's Financial Accounting Standards Board (DSAK IAI) before stating that a new international treatment applies locally. Cash-equivalent criteria in Indonesia are set out in PSAK 207 Statement of Cash Flows, the counterpart of IAS 7 (see also our discussion of the IASB proposal on cash equivalents).

DSAK IAI Implementation Bulletin Volume 2, in discussing investments in shares or money market fund units redeemable at any time, notes that under paragraph 7 of PSAK 207 the purpose of holding cash equivalents is to meet short-term cash commitments. An instrument must be convertible into a determinable amount of cash and be subject to an insignificant risk of changes in value; being redeemable at any time is not enough. That discussion is not about stablecoins and does not mean IAI has decided their classification. The analogy only helps frame the questions, while a conclusion on a particular stablecoin requires analysis of rights, redemption terms, liquidity, and risk under the applicable standards.

Practical Steps for Holders of Cryptoassets

Entities holding cryptoassets should build an inventory of instruments by their nature, not by their marketing names:

  1. Group assets by nature: cryptocurrencies with no issuer and no contract, stablecoins that give a claim against an issuer, and other tokens.
  2. Record the purpose of holding: held for sale in the ordinary course of business, broker-trader activity, or another purpose.
  3. For stablecoins, keep documents on the redemption mechanism, the party promising payment, and any restrictions.
  4. Determine the data sources for measurement and who reviews them.
  5. Document the chosen policy and its rationale, then monitor the IASB project page and DSAK IAI announcements regularly.

In the financial statements, avoid giving the impression that plans to develop standards have changed the amounts presented. Explain the policies currently used, the significant judgements affecting classification and measurement, and the relevant uncertainties under the applicable standards. Where holdings are diverse, grouping them by rights and purpose of holding is more useful to readers than a single broad “cryptoassets” label. If the IASB later publishes a consultation document or a final standard, only then check its scope, effective date, transition provisions, and the adoption process in Indonesia based on official sources.

Conclusion

The IASB's decision in November 2026 marks the start of a targeted discussion of holdings of cryptocurrencies and stablecoins, not a final change in accounting treatment. The June 2019 agenda decision of the IFRS Interpretations Committee remains a reference for the fact pattern it addressed. For entities in Indonesia, the work now is to understand the instruments held, apply the applicable SAK, document judgements, and monitor official developments before changing policies.
