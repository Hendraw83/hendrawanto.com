Reading PPATK Fund Turnover Figures in Financial Tracing

Why transaction value, suspected criminal proceeds, seized assets, and state loss must be kept apart

By Hendrawanto, SE., Ak., CA., CLI., ASEAN CPA, CPA., CFI. · 20 November 2026 · Baca dalam Bahasa Indonesia

News about tracing money flows often features large figures that are easily read as “the amount of criminal proceeds”. Yet the terms used by official sources can differ. In a news release published on 19 November 2026, Indonesia’s Financial Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre (PPATK) reported the discovery of fund turnover of up to Rp2.9 trillion in the account of an iron trading company suspected of being where narcotics proceeds were mixed with business transactions. The same release states that investigators seized assets of about Rp150 billion based on interim asset tracing. The two figures mean different things, and neither is presented as a state financial loss. This article explains how to read them without judging the guilt of any party.

Fund Turnover Describes Activity, Not an End Result

According to PPATK’s release of 19 November 2026, which reports a joint press conference with the National Police Criminal Investigation Agency (Bareskrim) on 18 November 2026, the Rp2.9 trillion figure is described as fund turnover in the company account analysed for the period 2018–2024. The release does not explain how each transaction was added up, how much of the account reflected legitimate business funds versus funds suspected of being linked to crime, or how the turnover figure reconciles with the assets later seized. Without those details, readers cannot turn a turnover figure into a figure for criminal proceeds simply by changing the label. The figure is most safely read as the value of transaction activity within the announced scope of the tracing.

In another publication, PPATK’s release of 14 December 2023 explained that the nominal values of analysis results presented at that time were an aggregate calculation of both incoming and outgoing funds in the accounts of the reported parties and their affiliates. That explanation comes from a different context and data set, so it does not prove that the latest figure was calculated in exactly the same way. It does show, however, why the definition of a transaction figure should be checked first.

If initial funds of Rp100 million move from account A to B and then to C, incoming funds across the three accounts total Rp300 million and outgoing funds Rp200 million, giving an aggregate of Rp500 million, even though the unique funds from the original source remain Rp100 million. This is not an additional finding about the reported case; it is a reason always to ask about definitions.

Suspected Criminal Proceeds Require Tracing to Their Origin

When one account receives business payments as well as funds suspected of coming from crime, the total of all account movements does not answer how much of it constitutes criminal proceeds. In PPATK’s release, the mixing of funds is said to have been detected through PPATK’s financial analysis, which investigators then followed up. The release does not state what portion of the Rp2.9 trillion is linked to the narcotics business. Identifying a transaction pattern is a starting point for testing that link, not a basis for assuming that every rupiah in the account has the same origin.

In practice, tracing requires a chain of evidence: account documents, verifiable identities of senders and recipients, the economic basis of each transaction, and consistency with the books or supporting documents. If funds leave an account and come back through another, the examiner needs to explain whether the event represents new value or a repetition of the same flow. This is a general testing framework; the public information in PPATK’s release does not allow readers to reconstruct the flows of any particular case.

Seized Assets and State Loss Are Different Categories

A figure for seized assets describes the objects subjected to seizure at the reported stage, not the total transactions of an account. Assets found may differ in form and value from the money that originally flowed. Seizure during an investigation is also not the same as a court decision on confiscation or asset recovery. PPATK’s release states that investigators, together with PPATK, are still examining transaction flows to trace possible additional assets. An interim seizure figure therefore cannot be used as a definitive ceiling on criminal proceeds, let alone equated with the fund turnover figure.

The difference in terms also matters when discussing state loss. The release that prompted this article concerns suspected money laundering linked to narcotics offences and asset tracing; it contains no calculation of state financial loss. Calling the turnover or seized-asset figure a “state loss” adds a conclusion the source does not give. In cases that do require a state loss calculation, the object, the legal authority, and the calculation method must be examined separately, as discussed in Standards for Calculating State Losses in Constitutional Court Case 206/PUU-XXIV/2026 and Reading BPK’s IHPS I 2026.

Where Does PPATK’s Analysis Sit in the Process?

In PPATK’s release of 17 September 2026, PPATK describes its financial intelligence work in three stages: receiving financial data, analysing transaction data and relationships between entities, and then sharing the analysis results with law enforcement and relevant authorities. Mapping money flows helps direct investigations and asset tracing, but news about analysis results is not the same as a judgment that settles the criminal facts.

In the latest release, investigators are said to have followed up PPATK’s analysis, taken enforcement action, and continued developing the case. Readers should therefore separate intelligence information, investigative steps, and the final outcome of the legal process. If intelligence figures are treated straight away as losses or proven criminal proceeds, the scale and status of a case are easily misunderstood. Conversely, care with terminology does not diminish the value of financial analysis: transaction patterns and links between accounts can point to evidence that no single document reveals.

Questions for Reading Figures Responsibly

When a release contains a turnover figure, ask the following:

  1. Which accounts and which period are covered?
  2. Does the figure include incoming funds, outgoing funds, or both?
  3. Are there repeated transfers between accounts that are counted more than once?
  4. What portion can already be linked to a particular source?
  5. Are the assets mentioned still the result of tracing, already seized, or already ruled on by a court?

Not every answer has to be available in a short news release. Where it is not, that limit on the information should be stated honestly rather than filled with assumptions.

Conclusion

PPATK’s release shows the value of a follow the money approach, as well as the importance of discipline in reading figures. Transaction turnover shows activity within the scope of analysis; suspected criminal proceeds require proof of origin and of transaction links; seized assets show action taken against specific objects at a stage of the process; and state loss is a separate category that was not calculated in the release. Keeping these boundaries makes public discussion more accurate and leaves room for further examination of the evidence.
