Reading Trade-Based Money Laundering Indicators Without Jumping to Conclusions

Trade documents, the flow of goods, and payment flows should be compared before an anomaly is assessed further

By Hendrawanto, SE., Ak., CA., CLI., ASEAN CPA, CPA., CFI. · 27 November 2026 · Baca dalam Bahasa Indonesia

Trade-based money laundering (TBML) exploits commercial activity that is legitimate on its face to disguise criminal proceeds and move economic value across borders. In a news release published on 26 November 2026, Indonesia’s Financial Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre (PPATK) stated that payment transactions, invoices, customs documents, and even international trade routes can be misused for that purpose. The statement was made at the regional workshop Strengthening National Responses to Trade-Based Money Laundering: From Detection to Action, held in Jakarta on 24–25 November 2026. This article discusses how to read TBML indicators carefully: as a reason to examine the facts, not as a verdict.

What the PPATK Workshop Covered

According to the PPATK release, the workshop was organised by PPATK with UNODC and the Asian Development Bank (ADB) as part of a collaboration between UNODC, ADB, and the Ministry of Finance of Luxembourg to strengthen the capacity of national authorities in Asia and the Pacific. Participants came from PPATK, the Financial Services Authority (OJK), the Ministry of Finance, the Indonesian National Police, the Ministry of Law, the Ministry of Trade, and financial service providers.

PPATK explained that trade-related and financial transaction information is scattered across various institutions, so tackling TBML requires cross-sector coordination. The discussion covered identification of trade and financial transaction information, reporting, information exchange, analysis, and follow-up. The workshop also discussed using trade information when preparing Suspicious Transaction Reports (LTKM) and the drafting of a National TBML Guidance and a TBML-STR Template tailored to Indonesia’s legal framework, risk profile, and operational needs. The release describes the guidance and template as being prepared; the source does not state that either has been issued.

Why the Data Must Be Compared

One party may hold only the contract and invoice, while another holds customs, shipping, or account data. A conclusion drawn from a single fragment risks missing a reasonable explanation—or, conversely, missing an anomaly that only appears once the data are compared. That is the core of PPATK’s message about scattered information.

An initial review can start with three questions: what goods or services were agreed, who is entitled to receive payment, and whether the amount paid matches the documents. Then match the dates, quantities, descriptions, and parties across the contract, invoice, proof of shipment, and payment records. A difference does not always mean a violation. Changed orders, payment through an agent, or administrative corrections may have legitimate explanations. The analyst’s task is to assess whether the explanation is supported by consistent evidence.

Risk context matters too. The summary of the 2021 Trade-Based Money Laundering Risk Assessment on PPATK’s website describes TBML as a complex phenomenon whose elements cross not only sectoral boundaries but also national borders. The assessment considers threat, vulnerability, and impact, and identifies risk based on modus operandi, indications of predicate offences, industry sectors, modes of transport, and red flags or suspicious financial transaction indicators. Because risk profiles differ by activity, reviews should follow the nature of the goods and the way the transaction is conducted, rather than applying the same suspicion to every exporter or importer.

The Four FATF–Egmont Indicator Groups

On 11 March 2021, the FATF and the Egmont Group published Trade-Based Money Laundering: Risk Indicators to help the public and private sectors identify possible TBML. It supplements the Trade-Based Money Laundering: Trends and Developments report published in December 2020. The indicators are grouped into four categories: the structure of the business, trade activity, trade documents and commodities, and account and transaction activity.

In practice, the questions in each category can be turned into concrete tests. On the document and goods side, for example: is the description of the goods specific enough to be assessed, do the quantity or quality on the invoice match the shipping evidence, have documents been amended repeatedly, and does the shipping route have a commercial rationale? Transaction values need careful reading. A price far from comparables is only meaningful if the type, quality, delivery terms, timing, and market are sufficiently comparable; goods without an open market price are naturally harder to assess.

On the account side, questions include: were payment instructions changed suddenly, does payment come from a party with no apparent link to the shipment, and is account activity consistent with the scale of the business? Group structures, authorised intermediaries, and trade-finance arrangements can explain certain patterns. Keep the sequence of events—when the obligation arose, when goods were shipped, when funds were received, and where they moved next—and do not equate the sum of account movements with net trade value, as discussed in Reading PPATK Fund Turnover Figures.

From Indicator to a Limited Conclusion

A single anomaly is rarely enough to conclude anything. For an analysis to be defensible, each finding should go through the following sequence:

  1. Record the indicator and its source document, for example a quantity difference between the invoice and the shipping evidence.
  2. Gather possible explanations: contract revisions, grouping of goods, payment through an agent, or administrative error.
  3. Verify those explanations with additional lawful evidence, not assumptions.
  4. State the data limits. Some comparators, such as customs data or market prices, may not be accessible to private parties; record them as open questions.
  5. Draw a limited conclusion that covers only what the evidence supports, then report in line with applicable obligations.

For reporting parties, such findings feed into an LTKM through the procedures and authorised officers required by the rules that bind them. An internal assessment or investigative finding does not replace the authority of law enforcement and the courts to determine whether an offence occurred and who is responsible.

Conclusion

TBML calls for reading trade documents, the flow of goods, and the flow of money together. PPATK stresses inter-agency work because key information is scattered, while the FATF–Egmont paper provides four indicator groups to help select transactions for review. Indicators should start questions and verification, not serve as verdicts. Clear documentation of what is known, what is not yet known, and why the next step is needed produces a more defensible analysis. The national guidance now being drafted is worth watching, as it may clarify expectations for reporting parties.
